# Common Ground — final pitch and judge notes

8 November 2026 · Shipped scope: adult browser game, teams of up to six, two players per mission, two playable maps. Rules and the complete research register: [team specification](team-spec.md). R1–R16 below refer to that register.

**For the actual presentation, use the shorter [timed six-slide script](pitch-script.md).** This document is the expanded evidence and Q&A reference. ## Positioning

**Common Ground is a cooperative game designed to support everyday communication and give lasting bonds a place to grow.** Two people coordinate robots through strategic missions; their achievements become part of a persistent world shared with their small team. The challenge comes from decisions and interdependence. Presentation and communication remain calm and configurable.

**Slide line:** “Shared challenges. Real communication. A world worth returning to.”

**Design line:** “Challenging through strategy. Calm by design.”

Noncompetitive means no player-versus-player race, leaderboard or individual social score. It does not mean automatic success: the pair can run out of rounds. Both the carrier and the person holding the plate receive contribution credit.

## Thirty-second version

Common Ground gives autistic adults a shared activity with a reason to communicate and a reason to return. Two teammates guide robots through a maze: one holds a pressure plate while the other retrieves a component, then they coordinate the return. They can use signals, map pings or optional text chat. Every completed mission fills part of their team's shared world and remembers who helped build it. It is strategic, untimed and calm by design, with purposeful communication inside the game and lasting connection as the goal.

## Three-minute spoken pitch

**Opening — show the gate and two robots**

“Can you hold this while I get through?”

That small request is part of everyday life. We ask for help, confirm a plan, wait for someone, and work out when it is safe to change what we are doing. Common Ground puts those interactions inside a shared challenge.

We are building for autistic adults who want a calmer, more predictable way to play with other people. Research with autistic adults describes the importance of acceptance, comfortable company and environments that fit their sensory needs. Connection matters; the conditions for taking part matter too. [R10, R11, R13, R15]

**Show the mission**

Two players control robots. This gate opens only while someone occupies its pressure plate. One player needs support to collect a component. Their partner needs to know when they can leave. They have limited energy and eight rounds to bring everything home, so agreeing on a plan changes whether they succeed.

A player can ask “Can you hold plate A?” Their teammate can answer Yes or No. They can mark a location, request energy, or choose free text. There is no correct conversational script and no score for sounding a particular way. Communication has a practical consequence between two real people.

The advanced mission introduces two plate-and-gate pairs. Players take turns supporting each other. It creates a reason to practise asking, responding and adjusting a shared plan.

**Show the world and contributor names**

Those two players belong to a team of up to six. Each success fills an unfinished part of their shared world, with both contributors credited. Later, another pair can continue. The world preserves “we made this together” and gives the same people a reason to come back. We want familiarity and mutual support to have room to develop into lasting bonds.

**Explain the deliberate calm**

The challenge is strategic: who moves, who stays, and when? Decisions are untimed. Sound starts off, motion is limited, and communication has several forms. Research has found differences in how autistic groups respond to repeated sounds; adult accounts also show that sensory preferences vary. We designed for choice rather than assuming that more stimulation means more engagement. [R13, R14]

If this looks quieter than the games you usually play, consider whose comfort those games assume. Inclusive design can change the environment people are invited into.

**Evidence and close**

Related supported game-based programmes have reported communication benefits beyond gameplay, including at home and school. Small adult simulation studies also support the possibility of transfer in a specific trained task. That supports our direction; it does not yet prove outcomes for Common Ground. [R2, R5, R6]

We have built the working cooperation mechanism and a persistent shared world. Our next step is testing with autistic adults whether people enjoy returning, feel comfortable communicating, and find the practice useful elsewhere.

Common Ground: shared challenges, real communication, and a world worth returning to.

## How gameplay connects to everyday communication

These are **practice opportunities and proposed analogues**, not observed transfer in our users. Players can use short signals throughout; free text and spontaneous speech are never required for success.

| Mission situation                                | Interpersonal action being practised                      | Everyday analogue                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| A gate needs someone else on the plate           | Make a specific request and identify the needed support   | “Can you hold the door while I carry this?”               |
| The other player is already doing a task         | Accept, decline, or renegotiate availability              | “I can't help now; can we do it after this?”              |
| A carrier needs to return before a holder leaves | Confirm a shared plan and coordinate a change             | Agree who is covering a task and when responsibility ends |
| Someone has insufficient energy                  | Express a practical need; respond without guessing intent | Ask for resources or assistance with a shared job         |
| A route fails or a plate is vacated too early    | Notice that assumptions differed and revise the plan      | Clarify a misunderstanding during a group activity        |
| Power station requires reciprocal support        | Switch between receiving help and providing it            | Reciprocal participation in a shared project              |

The game checks positions, resources, actions and completion. It does not evaluate the quality of a person's language. More messages do not necessarily mean better communication; a well-understood ping can be enough.

## How the design makes room for lasting bonds

**Design pathway:** chosen team → repeated shared goals → opportunities to help each other → visible shared history → reasons to meet again.

Example: two teammates restore the greenhouse and both names remain beneath it. Another pair restores the power station later. The group has a shared artifact that records everyone's contribution, including the less visible work of supporting someone else's route. Pair missions keep each interaction small while the world belongs to the wider group.

Adult relationship research supports the importance of comfort, acceptance and trust [R10–R11]; board-game research supports shared activities as a context for social connection [R7]. Maintaining a team is our design inference from that evidence. It has not been experimentally shown that these team sizes or contributor labels produce deeper bonds. Friendship remains voluntary, and someone can end an attempt if their partner does not respond. Do not describe the system as holding people accountable for being available.

## Demo run of show

**Before judging:** use a dedicated demo team and two browser identities. Two tabs at the same origin use the same identity; use separate browser profiles or the documented localhost/127.0.0.1 pair for the local demo. For two devices or judges, use the public deployment at https://common-ground.sodeeqalli.workers.dev. It has a fresh, separate database, so create the demo team there rather than reusing a local invite code. The earlier managed Site remains owner-private.

1. Show the team and unfinished world. Say what is saved and point out that two people take this mission together.
2. Start Greenhouse. The second player's screen should enter the mission automatically. Choose different jobs and ready both players.
3. Send “Can you hold plate A?” and answer Yes on the other screen. Point out that No and optional free text are also available. Place one meaningful map ping, not a burst of notifications.
4. Move the holder onto A and show the gate change. Move the carrier through while the holder uses Stay. Explain that leaving the plate early would close the gate; if demonstrating a bump, use a separate attempt so the main run remains solvable.
5. Finish the retrievals and return both robots home. Show the coloured greenhouse and both contributors, then reload once to show persistence.
6. Open Power station only if time permits: point out A/B and why both partners must take turns providing support.

A complete eight-round mission is longer than a short pitch. Rehearse its timing. For a brief demo, play a real attempt to the relevant gate position beforehand and clearly call it a prepared checkpoint; show the final restoration using a recording of the complete genuine run or a second genuinely completed team. Do not imply skipped rounds happened live or fabricate saved progress.

### Reproducible Greenhouse walkthrough

Both start at home. Player A chooses Water pump; player B chooses Control module. Each row is one round's route, with both players clicking through the coordination/readiness stages. U/D/L/R are the movement buttons. Do not spend or transfer extra energy in this particular sequence.

| Round | Player A | Player B | What to explain                                   |
| ----- | -------- | -------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | U U L    | U U      | Positioning and saving B's unused movement energy |
| 2     | Stay     | R R R U  | B travels toward their component                  |
| 3     | Stay     | U D D    | B collects the control module and returns         |
| 4     | Stay     | L L L    | B reaches plate A                                 |
| 5     | L L U    | Stay     | B supports A's access through the gate            |
| 6     | U D D    | Stay     | A collects the pump and returns through the gate  |
| 7     | R R R    | Stay     | A clears the gated route                          |
| 8     | D D      | D D      | Both deliver and return home                      |

The holder's unused energy is discarded on Stay. There is no wall-clock penalty for discussing the plan. This is one working strategy, not a required script for players.

## Judge questions

**“Does this improve real-world communication?”**

It gives players repeated opportunities to coordinate with an actual person. The intervention precedents in R2 and R5–R6 give a credible reason to investigate transfer. We have not yet measured it in Common Ground. Our next evaluation separates better game play from communication in a different shared task and participant-defined daily situations.

**“Is most of your evidence about children?”**

Much of the intervention literature is. The review in R4 included 20 studies, 19 involving children/adolescents. We use adult play, gaming, friendship and sensory studies for audience/design choices [R7–R15], and the adult interview work only as narrow transfer precedent. We do not treat a child's supported programme as proof for an adult standalone game.

**“Why not just play Minecraft or It Takes Two?”**

They can be excellent choices, including for autistic players. We are testing a more bounded format: two people have a clear shared task, can choose low-demand signals or text, take time to plan, and contribute to the same small team's visible history. The contribution is that combined design for this audience, rather than claiming to have invented cooperation or belonging.

**“Why is it so quiet? Will it be boring?”**

There is still a strategy problem and a real loss condition. We put difficulty in interdependence and resource decisions, while reducing unnecessary sensory events. Research supports varied sensory needs, not a universal preference for quiet [R13–R16]. Whether this balance is enjoyable is something we must learn from players; a quieter interface alone is not evidence of engagement.

**“Why keep teams rather than randomly match people each time?”**

Repeated partners and visible joint accomplishments make continuity possible. Research about adult relationships emphasizes acceptance and trust [R10–R11], which motivates giving relationships room to develop over time. We have not shown that persistent teams cause deeper bonds, and a person should be free to stop an attempt or choose another partner.

**“Is structured messaging infantilizing or forced?”**

The controls communicate useful game actions, and are available to everyone. They are optional supports alongside map pings and opt-in text chat. There is no diagnosis test, no requirement to use a particular style, and no reward for sounding neurotypical.

**“What if a partner disappears?”**

There is no response timer. Either mission participant can end that attempt with confirmation, retain completed world progress and start again with another teammate. We do not have automated availability matching or a replacement bot.

**“What is the technical core?”**

An authoritative deterministic engine resolves both players' routes one step at a time. Plate occupancy changes precede gate-entry checks, including simultaneous arrivals and departures. The same mission definitions drive preview and server resolution. Versioned D1 writes and request IDs protect concurrent updates and duplicate requests; clients poll for changes. Success, restored regions and contributor credits persist in one state update. The complexity is reliable interdependent multiplayer state, not an AI response generator.

**“What is actually built?”**

Greenhouse, Power station, server-backed team persistence, opt-in discovery, pair selection, signals with responses, map pings, actionable energy requests, optional text chat, comfort controls, world restoration and contributor credits. Bridge and Workshop are placeholders. The eight authored conversations are archived; there is no Practice or Race mode in the shipped UI.

**“What would you do next?”**

Invite autistic adults into design and evaluation. First check whether play and communication are comfortable and enjoyable; then study repeated-session connection and transfer outside the maze. Product work after the hackathon would include account recovery, community safeguards and replayable content. Those are future work, not features to claim in this demo.

## Evidence and claim discipline

Use the [full R1–R16 register](team-spec.md#research-basis-and-claims) for samples, methods, findings, direct sources and limits. Distinguish four statements:

1. **Built:** teammates must coordinate actions and can return to saved shared progress.
2. **Research-supported rationale:** structured play and appropriately adapted environments can support participation and practice; some related supported interventions report outside-game benefits.
3. **Intended impact:** more comfortable everyday coordination and opportunities for lasting bonds.
4. **Not measured here:** improvements in everyday communication, reduced loneliness, deeper friendships, clinical efficacy or enjoyment for the target population.

Do not turn an effect size into a percentage improvement, add overlapping study samples, imply autistic adults co-designed this build, or claim neurodivergent people all process input the same way. Keep source qualifications in the appendix/Q&A and one clear sentence in the spoken pitch; the rest of the presentation should demonstrate the working product.
